Part A · The lesson, with your numbers
IDEA 1 · THE QUESTION

Picking alpha without peeking

Ridge, Lasso and ElasticNet each have a dial called alpha. The model can't learn alpha from the data; you choose it. Those settings are called hyperparameters. The whole notebook asks one question: how do you choose alpha without using the test set? The test set is your final exam. If you pick the setting that scores best on it, the score stops being an honest prediction for new data.

Your notebook answers the question four times, each time a bit better:

IDEA 2 · REGULARIZATION

A fine for big coefficients

Plain linear regression only tries to make the errors small. Regularization adds a fine for large coefficients, and alpha sets how big that fine is:

Ridge:  error + α × (β₁² + β₂² + β₃²)        squares: shrinks everything, rarely to 0
Lasso:  error + α × (|β₁| + |β₂| + |β₃|)     absolute values: pushes weak features to exactly 0

IDEA 3 · WHY SCALE FIRST

Scale before you fine

The fine is on the size of a coefficient, and that size depends on the unit. Measure TV in thousands instead of units and its coefficient grows 1,000 times, so its fine does too. Scaling puts every feature at mean 0 and standard deviation 1, so the fine treats them equally.

fit

Learns the mean and standard deviation. Only on X_train: TV mean 151.66, std 84.26 in your notebook.

transform

Applies those training numbers. On X_train and on X_test. The test set never gets its own mean.

If you fit the scaler on all the data, the test rows have leaked into training a little. Lesson 6 is about exactly that problem (data leakage).

IDEA 4 · CROSS-VALIDATION

Five small exams

A single validation set of 30 rows can be lucky or unlucky. With 5-fold cross-validation, the 140 training rows are cut into 5 folds of 28. Each fold takes one turn as the validation set while the model trains on the other 4. You get 5 scores and average them.

Average MSE:

Look at the spread for α=100: one fold says 4.94, another 11.40. A single validation split could have given you either number.

Why the scores are negative

cross_val_score and GridSearchCV always pick the highest score. For MSE lower is better, so scikit-learn flips the sign: scoring='neg_mean_squared_error'. −3.34 beats −8.22. Take abs() or multiply by −1 when you report it.

IDEA 5 · GRIDSEARCHCV

Every combination, five times

GridSearchCV is the loop from Idea 4 done for you. You hand it a model, a dictionary of values to try and a CV setting. It tries every combination with cross-validation, keeps the best, and then retrains that one on all the training data.

param_grid = {'alpha':    [0.1, 1, 5, 10, 50, 100],          # 6 values
              'l1_ratio': [.1, .5, .7, .9, .95, .99, 1]}      # 7 values
                                     6 × 7 = 42 combinations × 5 folds = 210 fits

ElasticNet

A mix of both fines. l1_ratio=1 is pure Lasso, l1_ratio close to 0 is almost pure Ridge.

After .fit()

best_params_ gives the winner, best_estimator_ the retrained model, cv_results_ every score.

It will be slow, and it may warn you

With 273 features, Lasso-heavy combinations take seconds each. On my run the 210 fits took about 100 seconds with n_jobs=-1 (uses all your CPU cores). You may also see a ConvergenceWarning: the solver stopped before it was finished. The fix is ElasticNet(max_iter=1000000), which gives it more steps. That also makes it slower, so start with a small grid to test your code.
